This paper argues that persistent corruption in Brazil occurs not only because of the weak institutional structure and individual moral failure; rather, it is better understood through a second-order collective action problem in which people, firms, and political actors lack incentives to enforce anti-corruption norms. This failure of enforcement is supported by asymmetric power structures, principal-agent problems, and socially internalized norms such as *jeitinho brasileiro*, which normalizes rule-bending behavior. Even though the majority of the population prefers lower levels of corruption in the state, coordination failure and unequal power distribution preserve a high-corruption equilibrium. Through a combination of game-theoretical analysis and cross-country empirical data, in this paper we will argue that effective anti-corruption reform requires the implementation of mechanisms that would alter the incentives for enforcement against corruption and the reshaping and establishment of better social norms.
