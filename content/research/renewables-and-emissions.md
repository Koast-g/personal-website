This paper examines whether positive shocks to renewable energy generation, specifically wind and solar power, causally reduce CO₂ emissions from coal and natural gas combustion in the U.S. power sector. The data was obtained from the U.S. Energy Information Administration and the FRED. Using monthly data from January 2000 to December 2025, I estimate a Structural Vector Autoregression model with a Cholesky identification strategy. Additionally, the model incorporates macroeconomic controls, weather-driven energy demand, and natural gas prices. Across all specifications, I find no statistical evidence that renewable generation shocks produce any response from CO₂ emissions. For natural gas, the full sample specification produces suggestive results but remains imprecise. However, during the structural break test, results reveal that renewable shocks generate an immediate and statistically significant reduction of approximately 1% in natural gas emissions, with renewable generation explaining up to 20% of natural gas emissions variance by month 24. Secondary results identify a consistent relationship between coal and gas substitution, highlighting the merit-order mechanism. Translating the displacement effect of renewables on natural gas emissions in the post-2012 period into economic terms using the Social Cost of Carbon of $21 per ton, the future avoided climate change damages are valued at approximately $89.1 million.
